Maryland–Virginia Prototype v0.1

Growth is a number.
Capacity is who gets to act.

The Capacity Distribution Index studies infrastructure development — starting with data centers — as a redistribution of capability among corporations, government, communities, workers, households, infrastructure systems, and future residents. A project can expand several capacities at once while quietly constraining others. Conventional measures flatten that into "growth." CDI keeps the arrows.

The Problem

Activity measures tell you how much happened.
They don't tell you who gained the ability to act.

Investment, tax revenue, employment, and electricity demand are real — and routinely reported. What's missing is the shift underneath them.

What conventional measures show

↑ Corporate expansion capacity

↑ Government technical capacity

↑ Grid capacity, construction employment

Reported as: "$X billion in investment, Y jobs created."

What goes unmeasured alongside it

↓ Available transmission headroom

↓ Land-use flexibility

↓ System legibility

Community bargaining power: ↔ unresolved, rarely asked

Core proposition None of these movements automatically make a project good or bad. The pattern itself — who gains, who loses, what dependency is created, what remains possible later — is the finding.
What CDI Measures

Seven capacity domains, one agency layer running through all of them

Agency is not an eighth domain — it's the lens applied across all seven, from first visibility to final remedy.

Evidence Confidence

A 75-C is not a 75-A

The same grading discipline MDCGT uses for VERIFIED / ASSESSMENT / UNKNOWN, applied to indicator-level scoring.

A

Directly measured, from an authoritative source — a filing, docket, or statute.

B

Strong proxy, or corroborated across multiple sources.

C

Incomplete or reconstructed evidence — assembled, not confirmed.

D

Exploratory qualitative judgment. Labeled as a judgment call, not a fact.

The Underlying Claim

CDI isn't measuring benefits and harms. It's measuring changes in the ability to act.

Five relational mechanisms — the bridge between Capacity Exchange and CDI's empirical instrument.

Capacity creation
An actor can do something it previously could not.
Capacity consumption
A finite capability is used up — transmission headroom, land, water.
Capacity transfer
One actor's expanded discretion reduces another's.
Capacity dependency
An actor gains a capability contingent on another actor continuing to provide something.
Capacity lock-in
Present action narrows the choices actually available later.
The testable proposition A public cannot meaningfully exercise agency over a system it cannot see as a system. Context Capacity — legibility, not just data availability — may be CDI's most important contribution.
